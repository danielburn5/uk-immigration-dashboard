Claims under examination

Does the evidence support the argument?

These verdicts assess specific testable statements, not political parties or people. “Not established” is different from “false”.

Contradicted by recent trend

“British citizens are leaving in ever-increasing numbers.”

What the figures show: An estimated 240,000 British nationals left in the year to June 2022, 257,000 in 2024 and 245,000 in 2026. Departures have fluctuated, not risen year after year. Important distinction: this does not mean British emigration is insignificant, and these estimates do not tell us why people left.

Supported

“The UK is seeing a growing net loss of British nationals.”

What the figures show: British net migration went from −67,000 in the year to June 2022 to −137,000 in 2026. The bigger change was fewer British nationals arriving (172,000 down to 109,000), rather than a surge in those leaving (240,000 versus 245,000). What we cannot tell: why fewer British nationals are moving to the UK.

Contradicted by recent trend

“Immigration is currently rising year after year.”

What the figures show: Total long-term immigration fell from about 1.47 million in the year to June 2023 to 738,000 in 2026—roughly half the peak. So this claim is wrong about the recent direction of immigration. Important context: the unusually large increase before 2023 was real too; a fall from that peak does not erase it.

Supported

“Immigration has shifted towards non-EU+ nationalities.”

What the figures show: In the year to June 2026, around 550,000 of 738,000 long-term arrivals—about 75%—were non-EU+ nationals. The composition is very different from earlier years, although changes in ONS estimation methods make some long-term comparisons less straightforward. What this does not mean: nationality says nothing by itself about ethnicity, skills, contribution or whether someone is 'desirable'.

Contradicted

“Most non-EU+ immigrants are asylum seekers.”

What the figures show: In the year to June 2026, about 73,000 of 550,000 non-EU+ arrivals were asylum-related—roughly 13%, not a majority. Work-related arrivals numbered about 135,000 and study-related arrivals 257,000, including dependants. Important distinction: these categories describe recorded reasons for immigration, not anyone's personal worth or future contribution.

Not established

“Immigration is causing British citizens to leave.”

What the figures show: People are arriving in and leaving the UK at the same time, but that is not evidence that one movement causes the other. What is missing: information about individual decisions to leave—such as work, family, housing or tax considerations. These ONS migration counts cannot confirm or rule out immigration as a factor for particular people.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · No evidence on individual motives
Supported

“The UK experienced a historically large immigration surge.”

What the figures show: Long-term immigration reached an estimated 1.47 million in the year to June 2023, well above earlier figures in the published series. That was a substantial surge and is a legitimate subject for policy debate. Important context: arrivals subsequently fell to 738,000 by June 2026, and ONS methodology changes limit some comparisons across 2021.

Not established

“The highest earners or most productive British citizens are leaving.”

What the figures show: We have estimates of how many British nationals leave, and their age distribution, but not a breakdown of their earnings, wealth or productivity. What is missing: reliable evidence on the economic characteristics of people leaving and how those compare with the British population. The migration counts cannot establish this claim or its opposite.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · No earnings or productivity breakdown
Supported

“Most non-EU+ immigrants are aged 16–44.”

What the figures show: Approximately 450,000 of 550,000 non-EU+ arrivals in the year to June 2026—about 82%—were aged 16–44. The two largest groups were ages 16–24 (198,000) and 25–34 (191,000). Important distinction: being in a younger age group does not establish whether someone works, studies or contributes more in tax than they receive in services.

Not established

“Immigration always benefits the UK economy.”

What the figures show: These ONS tables count people arriving and leaving, including broad reasons for migration. They do not measure wages, taxes paid, public spending or overall economic effects. Verdict: the word 'always' cannot be justified from this dataset. A meaningful assessment would need additional economic evidence and would have to distinguish between different types and levels of immigration.

ONS spreadsheet, Tables 1 and 4b · Migration flows and reasons, not economic impact
Not established

“Immigration doesn't put pressure on housing or public services.”

What the figures show: Migration adds people to the population, but these tables do not tell us whether the supply of housing, schools or healthcare keeps pace—or how migrants contribute through work, taxes and service provision. Verdict: this blanket claim is not established here, and neither is the opposite assertion that immigration inevitably overwhelms services. Local capacity and wider ONS evidence would matter.

Not established

“The UK's ageing population makes current immigration levels necessary.”

What the figures show: A large majority of non-EU+ arrivals in the year to June 2026 were aged 16–44. That may matter for debates about the working-age population, but age is not the same as employment or long-term settlement. What is missing: a demographic and economic assessment of how much immigration would be needed under different policies. These age tables cannot establish that today's level is necessary.
