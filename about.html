A note about this project

Why I built this

I wanted to look beyond the headlines and understand what the UK's migration figures actually say.

This project started when I came across a graphic using the latest UK immigration statistics. It highlighted how many British citizens were leaving the country, alongside how many non-EU nationals were arriving. The figures were real, but the framing seemed designed to tell a much bigger story: that British people were leaving and being replaced by people who were somehow less welcome or less desirable.

I found that troubling. Not because immigration is beyond criticism, or because the numbers should be ignored, but because a few carefully selected statistics can be made to imply things they simply don't establish.

I wanted to know: are the claims supported by the evidence, or are we being encouraged to reach conclusions that the evidence doesn't justify?

The numbers deserve context

A single year's figures cannot tell us whether a trend is accelerating, reversing or staying broadly flat. Gross immigration and emigration aren't the same as net migration. Nationality isn't a measure of someone's ethnicity, skills, contribution or character. And the fact that two things happen at the same time doesn't prove one caused the other.

Looking at changes over several years gives a much clearer picture. It can also reveal facts that challenge my own assumptions. The substantial post-pandemic increase in immigration, for example, was real. So is the shift towards non-EU immigration. Those are legitimate subjects for debate, not statistics to explain away.

Not a case for one political side

I don't want this website to become a mirror image of the material that prompted it. The goal isn't to prove that the political right is always wrong, or that a more liberal view of immigration is automatically correct.

It's to make specific claims testable. Where the ONS data supports an argument, the website should say so. Where it contradicts a claim, it should explain why. And where the evidence is insufficient—especially on people's motives, economic value or the causes of migration—it should be comfortable saying we don't know from these figures.

I also think it's important to challenge the suggestion that someone's nationality makes them inherently desirable or undesirable. That is a value judgement, not a conclusion one can draw from a migration spreadsheet.

Why only the ONS?

For this initial project I've deliberately limited the numerical evidence to the Office for National Statistics. That gives readers a consistent, independently published starting point and makes the figures easier to check themselves.

This doesn't mean ONS estimates are perfect or that the statistics answer every policy question. The figures can be provisional, revised, and affected by changes in estimation methods. Those limitations matter and should be made clear rather than hidden.

What I hope people take from it

I hope this is useful in conversations where a striking graphic or statistic is being shared without much context. You don't have to agree with my political outlook to use it. You should be able to inspect the graphs, look at the underlying numbers, follow the source and make up your own mind.

Immigration policy is complicated. People can reasonably disagree about its scale and effects. But those disagreements should begin with an accurate understanding of what the data does—and doesn't—tell us.

This is a personal project, not an ONS publication or an endorsement by the ONS. The dashboard uses ONS data and links to the original release.
