The short version

Three things the headline doesn't tell you

Arrivals are down sharply.

Immigration fell from 1.47m in the year to June 2023 to 738k in June 2026 — roughly a halving.

British departures aren't surging.

245k British nationals left in 2026, compared with 240k in 2022. The bigger change is fewer arriving.

Most non-EU+ arrivals come for work or study.

392k out of 550k non-EU+ arrivals in 2026 were work- or study-related, including dependants.

Where are arrivals from?

Arrivals by nationality group · years ending June 2021–2026 · thousands

What stands out: Non-EU+ nationals accounted for 550k of 738k arrivals in 2026 (about 75%). This measures nationality groups, not ethnicity or individual circumstances.

Why do non-EU+ nationals come to the UK?

Work and study account for 392,000 of 550,000 non-EU+ arrivals in 2026, including dependants. Asylum accounts for 73,000 (about 13%). These are reasons for immigration, not assessments of people's character or contribution.

Claims under examination

Does the evidence support the argument?

These verdicts assess specific testable statements, not political parties or people. “Not established” is different from “false”.

Contradicted by recent trend

“British citizens are leaving in ever-increasing numbers.”

British emigration was 240k in 2022, peaked at 257k in 2024 and was 245k in 2026. There is no sustained recent acceleration. That does not mean the number leaving is unimportant.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · British emigration, YE June 2022–2026
Supported

“The UK is seeing a growing net loss of British nationals.”

British net migration shifted from −67k in 2022 to −137k in 2026. This reflects declining British immigration as well as departures. The reasons for that decline are not established here.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · British immigration, emigration and net migration
Contradicted by recent trend

“Immigration is currently rising year after year.”

Total immigration fell from 1.466m in 2023 to 738k in 2026. This does not negate the exceptionally large post-pandemic increase that preceded the decline.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · All nationalities
Supported

“Immigration has shifted towards non-EU+ nationalities.”

Non-EU+ nationals accounted for about 75% of long-term immigration in 2026. This is a real change in the composition of flows. Nationality does not establish ethnicity, skills or desirability.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · Immigration by nationality group
Contradicted

“Most non-EU+ immigrants are asylum seekers.”

Of 550k non-EU+ arrivals in 2026, 73k were classified as asylum-related (around 13%). Work and study were much larger categories: 135k and 257k.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 4b · Non-EU+ immigration reason
Not established

“Immigration is causing British citizens to leave.”

These data show how many people move, not why individuals decide to move. Simultaneous immigration and emigration cannot on their own establish causation.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · No evidence on individual motives
Supported

“The UK experienced a historically large immigration surge.”

Yes. The ONS estimates 1.466m long-term arrivals in the year to June 2023, a major increase from earlier years in the series. The subsequent fall is also part of the story.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · All nationalities
Not established

“The highest earners or most productive British citizens are leaving.”

The headline migration series contains no breakdown of British emigrants' pay, wealth or productivity. Making that claim would require different evidence.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · No earnings or productivity breakdown
Supported

“Most non-EU+ immigrants are young adults.”

In the year ending June 2026, approximately 450,000 of 550,000 non-EU+ arrivals (82%) were aged 16–44. The largest age bands were 16–24 (198,000) and 25–34 (191,000). Being of working age does not tell us whether someone is employed, studying or economically active.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 7d · Non-EU+ immigration by age
Not established

“Immigration always benefits the UK economy.”

The migration figures measure movement into and out of the UK, not the net fiscal or economic effects of those movements. The ONS migration tables cannot establish that every type or level of immigration has a positive economic effect.

ONS spreadsheet, Tables 1 and 4b · Migration flows and reasons, not economic impact
Not established

“Immigration doesn't put pressure on housing or public services.”

These migration counts cannot show whether added demand is matched by housing supply, public-service capacity or migrants' contributions to those services. The opposite claim—that immigration necessarily overwhelms services—also cannot be demonstrated from these tables alone.

ONS spreadsheet, Table 1 · Does not measure housing or service capacity
Not established

“The UK's ageing population makes current immigration levels necessary.”

The ONS age tables show that most non-EU+ arrivals in June 2026 were aged 16–44, which may be relevant to discussions about an ageing population. But these tables do not establish how much immigration is needed, or whether current levels are necessary or sufficient.

Data integrity

Sources and limitations

Every numeric figure on this page comes from the supplied ONS November 2026 publication spreadsheet, not newspapers, social media or political campaigns.

How to read these charts

  • Long-term means moving country of usual residence for at least 12 months; not all border crossings or all visas.
  • Each point is a year ending in June, not a calendar year. Consecutive points have overlapping 12-month coverage.
  • ONS values are rounded to thousands. Component totals and net differences may not sum exactly.
  • Immigration means arrivals, emigration means departures; net migration is the difference.

What to treat with caution

  • The ONS cautions against directly comparing British estimates before and after June 2021 because of methodology changes (Notes 16–17).
  • Wider time-series comparisons across June 2021 also warrant caution.
  • The ONS flags the June 2021 British emigration value as an explainable outlier; the British chart starts at June 2022 (Note 19).
  • Latest figures are provisional and subject to revision; some British data revisions were delayed (Cover Sheet; Note 22).
  • These figures alone cannot prove why people migrate or their economic effects.
